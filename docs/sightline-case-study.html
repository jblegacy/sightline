The premise

Job searching gets described as a volume problem, so the tooling built for it optimizes volume — auto-apply bots, mass submission, hundreds of applications a week. That tooling is now widely available, which means volume has stopped being an advantage and started being noise that everyone contributes to.

The actual constraints are different. You never see most of the roles you'd be a fit for, because they're posted on company ATS boards that aggregators index late or not at all. And when you do see one, you're often applying a week after it went live, with material that wasn't written for it.

So I built for those two constraints instead: see everything relevant within 24 hours, and arrive with material that matches the posting. Discovery, screening, and assembly are automated. Submission is not — and that boundary is the part of this I'd most want to talk about.

Where the boundary sits, and why

Every line on the right was a decision, not a limitation.

Automated

  • IngestionOne adapter per ATS type. Company-native boards first — they're freshest and least polluted.
  • DeduplicationNormalized on company, title, and location. Cuts roughly a third.
  • Deterministic filteringHard constraints resolved before any model call. Cheaper and it can't hallucinate.
  • Rubric scoringOne batched model call per posting, seven weighted dimensions, written rationale required.
  • Keyword extractionVerbatim phrases from the posting, since ATS keyword matching is literal.
  • Variant assemblySelects and orders from an approved bullet library. Never writes a new claim.
  • TrackingStatus, follow-up timers, response rate by variant.

Deliberately not automated

  • SubmissionATS terms prohibit it, the major platforms detect it, and a flag in a shared instance can follow you to every company using it.
  • Free-text answers"Why this company" is where a generic answer does more damage than no application at all.
  • Outreach copyTemplated cold outreach at volume is the most detectable thing in this market, and it burns the contact permanently.
  • New resume claimsSee below. This one I learned the hard way.
  • The final readIf I haven't read the job description, I don't want the job enough to be applying for it.

The provenance rule

The failure that shaped the design

While drafting a resume, a bullet appeared describing an internal system as built and deployed. I had specified it in detail. I had not shipped it. Nothing in my notes distinguished those two states — six weeks on, an ambitious plan and a running system read identically — and the bullet was one screen-share question away from being indefensible.

So every claim in the library carries a provenance tag, and a validator refuses to assemble a document that violates it. Ten lines of code. It's the most valuable thing in the repository.

TagMeaningShips
measuredInstrumented, with a number I can point atYes
statedAsserted by me, defensible from a documentYes
modeledMy estimate or projectionQualified only
derivedInferred by a tool, including by a modelNever

The general lesson generalizes past resumes: when a language model summarizes your own notes back to you, it will quietly promote intention to accomplishment, and it will do it in your voice. Systems that put model output in front of people need a layer that asks where each claim came from. That belief is now load-bearing in how I'd deploy this anywhere else.

How it's built

Ingestion

One adapter per ATS type behind a shared interface, so a breaking change is contained to one file. Nightly cron. Content hashing catches edited postings — a quietly added salary band is worth knowing about.

Scoring

Deterministic filters run first and remove most volume for free. Survivors go to the Batch API. The rubric was validated against twenty postings I'd already judged by hand before I trusted it.

Store

SQLite. Every stage writes to an append-only event log, so three weeks later I can still answer why the system surfaced something. No vector database — the corpus is a few hundred rows.

Assembly

Tailoring selects from a tagged library of pre-approved claims and renders to ATS-clean .docx. Output is verified by extracting the text back out and checking it parses in reading order.

Review

A local queue showing score, rationale, the diffed variant, and a one-page brief with gaps to prepare for. Approvals, edits, and rejections are logged with reasons.

Feedback

Rejection reasons at the gate are the training data for the next rubric revision. Where the scorer and I disagree repeatedly, one of us is wrong and the log says which.

What I'd say about it in an interview

On restraint. The version of this that auto-submits was three hours of additional work. I didn't build it, because the failure mode isn't wasted effort — it's a detection flag in a shared ATS instance that quietly costs you every company on that platform. Most AI deployment decisions look like that: the capability is trivial and the question is entirely about where the blast radius lands.

On scope. There's no vector store, no agent framework, and no orchestration layer beyond a scheduler, because the corpus is a few hundred rows and keyword matching plus one model call is the right answer. Choosing the boring architecture is a skill and it's badly underrepresented in AI project work right now.

On what I'd change. I built tailoring before I'd validated the scorer, which meant polishing output from a screener I hadn't checked. Discovery and screening carried nearly all the value; tailoring was a convenience I reached for early because it was more fun to build.

On the numbers. Response-rate-by-variant is directional, not statistical — the sample is small and confounded by company, timing, and role. I track it because it beats guessing, and I'd rather say that plainly than present it as a finding.
